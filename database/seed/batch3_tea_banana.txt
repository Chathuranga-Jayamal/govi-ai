## tea___algal_leaf_spot

Tea (Camellia sinensis) can suffer from algal leaf spot, an epiphytic disease caused by green algae (Cephaleuros spp.) that grow on leaf surfaces. Infected leaves show small (2–8 mm), round or irregular rusty-orange to brown spots that often develop a powdery or velvety green coating as they mature. These spots typically induce early yellowing and defoliation of affected leaves. The algae thrive in warm, humid environments (such as shaded, poorly ventilated tea fields) and spread via water splashes or wind. 

Management focuses on reducing leaf wetness and using appropriate sprays. Ensure good air circulation: prune dense canopies, avoid overhead irrigation, and keep plants well-spaced and sunny. Remove and destroy badly infected foliage. For chemical control, apply a copper-based fungicide (e.g. copper oxychloride) or a contact fungicide like mancozeb (at roughly 0.2–0.3% solution) to tea foliage. Thorough coverage of upper and lower leaf surfaces is important. As prevention, improve drainage and sanitation, do not over-fertilize with nitrogen, and monitor fields during rainy spells. If left unchecked, algal leaf spot can noticeably reduce yields (reported losses are up to ~5–15% in tea), so timely action in humid seasons is recommended.

## tea___anthracnose

Anthracnose of tea (often called brown blight) is a fungal leaf disease (Colletotrichum spp.) affecting tea shoots. It appears as water-soaked yellowish-green spots on young leaves that expand into deep brown or black necrotic patches. These lesions may have diffuse yellow margins or concentric rings, and black fungal fruiting bodies (acervuli) often form on older spots. New shoots infected by anthracnose may wilt or die back. Warm, rainy conditions favor this fungus, which can spread rapidly in dense, shaded canopies.

Control involves foliar fungicide sprays and sanitation. Use a broad-spectrum fungicide – for example, alternated applications of a protectant (such as chlorothalonil or mancozeb at ~0.2–0.3% concentration) with a systemic (e.g. carbendazim 0.05% or another benzimidazole). Spray at intervals (e.g. every 10–14 days) until lesions halt, ensuring full coverage of foliage. Remove and burn or deeply bury infected leaves and twigs to reduce inoculum. Cultural measures help prevent outbreaks: avoid excessive shade and crowding, prune to increase airflow, and maintain balanced nutrition so plants are less succulent. Tea anthracnose can defoliate bushes if severe; control promptly once symptoms appear to protect yield, though it is generally less urgent than diseases like blister blight.

## tea___bird_eye_spot

Bird’s eye spot in tea is caused by the fungus *Cercospora theae*. On leaves it produces distinctive round necrotic spots: the centers turn brown to black and are surrounded by a reddish margin. These spots typically appear on mature leaves; heavily infected leaves turn yellow and may drop. The disease favors warm, humid weather and spreads via rain splash and wind. Poor drainage, dense shading or mechanical injury (e.g. careless plucking) can exacerbate infection.

To manage bird’s eye spot, promptly remove and destroy heavily spotted leaves to reduce spread. Spraying with a copper-based fungicide (such as copper oxychloride) or other broad-spectrum fungicide can suppress the fungus. Apply thoroughly to cover all foliage, and repeat sprays at 10–14 day intervals if needed. Providing good growing conditions is crucial: ensure bushes have proper shade and drainage and do not become waterlogged. Adequate potassium nutrition may improve resistance. In practice, bird’s eye spot can cause serious defoliation of mature tea, especially with frequent mechanical harvesting: foliar diseases including this have been linked to yield losses of over 40% if unchecked. Early action when spots first appear is therefore advised.

## tea___brown_blight

Tea brown blight (also referred to as anthracnose or Colletotrichum blight) causes irregular dark lesions on new leaves and shoots. Initially, small water-soaked, yellow-green spots form; these enlarge into deep brown or black necrotic patches often with a faint yellowish-green border and occasional concentric rings. Black acervuli (fungal fruiting bodies) may be visible in the centers of old lesions. The disease thrives in warm, wet conditions and can quickly spread through a canopy.

Treatment relies on fungicides and sanitation. Apply a suitable foliar fungicide regime: for example, alternate a contact product like mancozeb (0.2–0.3% solution) with a systemic product like carbendazim (0.05%). Ensure sprays fully wet both sides of the leaves. Remove and burn badly infected shoots. Culturally, avoid excessive shade and crowding, and space plants to improve airflow. Maintaining moderate shade (so plants are healthy but not excessively humid) and avoiding over-watering or dense shade reduce outbreaks. Without control, brown blight can cause significant leaf loss and reduce yield, so manage promptly when first symptoms appear.

## tea___gray_blight

Gray blight on tea is a fungal leaf spot disease (associated with *Pestalotiopsis*-like fungi). It starts as numerous small brown spots on older leaves; over time the centers turn grayish and may display faint concentric rings, while the margins remain brown. As lesions coalesce, large gray-brown blotches can develop. The disease is favored by high humidity and warm temperatures, and is often seen during and after the rainy season.

Control of gray blight is similar to other tea foliar blights. Apply protective fungicides (e.g. a tank mix of mancozeb 0.2–0.3% followed by a systemic fungicide) to all foliage as soon as spots are noticed. Remove and destroy infected leaves to lower the inoculum. Improve ventilation in the field by thinning canopy and pruning neighboring vegetation. Ensure proper field sanitation by clearing old leaf debris where the fungus can overwinter. Balanced fertilization (avoiding excessive nitrogen) also helps plant health. Gray blight rarely kills a plant outright but can weaken bushes over time; treat significant outbreaks quickly to prevent widespread defoliation.

## tea___red_leaf_spot

Red leaf spot (often called “red rust”) of tea is caused by algal pathogens (Cephaleuros parasiticus and related species). Infected leaves develop small (5–7 mm), reddish-orange spots on the surface. These spots eventually turn pale or whitish as the algae die, giving a mottled or variegated appearance. Heavy infection can cause yellowing and drying of leaves, and even cracking of stems. The algae spread in warm, humid conditions (common in wet-zone tea estates) and tend to accumulate under shaded, damp canopies.

Management targets the algae. Regularly remove and destroy symptomatic leaves. Foliar sprays of copper-based fungicides (for example, copper oxychloride) are effective at suppressing Cephaleuros. Apply treatments when foliage is wet (so the copper sticks) during the rainy season for best effect. Cultural prevention is important: prune tea to open the canopy, ensuring good sunlight and airflow. Avoid prolonged leaf wetness (use drip rather than overhead irrigation). This disease tends to be chronic but usually causes only modest yield loss; severe outbreaks are not common. Routine sanitation and copper sprays in problem areas will keep it in check.

## tea___white_spot

White spot of tea (white scab disease) is a foliar fungus (Elsinoe leucospila). Early symptoms are small, reddish spots on leaves with grayish-white to grayish-brown centers. These expand into round necrotic lesions (“bird’s eye” shaped) with pale centers. In heavy infections the leaf may become distorted or die. The disease occurs in humid, high-altitude tea regions and can affect young flushes as well as mature leaves.

Control methods include sanitation and fungicides. Prune and destroy affected young shoots and leaves. Apply protective fungicides when flush is developing – for example, spray mancozeb or chlorothalonil (at 20–30 g per 10 L water) on new growth. Repeat as needed during wet weather. Good field hygiene (removing leaf litter) and avoiding adjacent plantings of susceptible tea also help. White spot usually progresses slowly, but if many leaves show lesions it can reduce plant vigor. Promptly treating any new outbreak prevents the spots from coalescing and causing severe defoliation.

## banana___cordana

Cordana leaf spot in banana, caused by the fungus *Neocordana musae* (formerly *Cordana musae*), produces large (often several cm) pale-brown, oval to fusiform lesions on older banana leaves. Spots typically have a pale gray concentric ring pattern and a dark brown border, and may be surrounded by a yellow halo. Lesions often begin on leaf margins or follow other wounds; they can coalesce into large necrotic patches in wet conditions. This disease is common in humid tropical areas and often appears late in the leaf life, but usually causes only mild loss of foliage under low disease pressure.

Management is mostly cultural. Remove and burn severely infected leaves to reduce inoculum. If chemical control is needed, spray a copper-based or Mancozeb contact fungicide early in the season – for example, chlorothalonil (500 g/L) or Mancozeb 75% (20 g per 10 L of water) applied thoroughly to new leaves. Repeat sprays as directed on the label, alternating with a systemic fungicide (such as propiconazole or tebuconazole at ~3.5 ml/10 L) if the disease is spreading. Preventive measures include using tolerant cultivars (Cavendish types tolerate Cordana best) and proper spacing (e.g. 8×8 ft) to improve airflow. Avoid overhead irrigation to keep foliage dry. Good sanitation by removing old hanging leaves and debris also helps. Under normal conditions, Cordana spot is only of minor importance, but control promptly if many leaves are affected to preserve canopy health.

## banana___pestalotiopsis

Pestalotiopsis leaf spot of banana is a fungal disease caused by *Pestalotiopsis* spp. It appears as small to medium irregular brown spots on the underside of older leaves. Centers may become grayish, and the spots may coalesce to cause extensive blotching. The pattern can resemble gray leaf speckle or black leaf streak, but often with slightly fuzzy margins. This fungus favors warm, humid conditions. 

Management parallels other banana leaf spots. Remove and destroy heavily infected leaves. Apply a contact fungicide such as Mancozeb (20 g/10 L) or copper oxychloride (40–50 g/10 L) to protect new leaves, and consider alternating with a systemic fungicide (e.g. a triazole at ~3.5 ml/10 L). Ensure sprays cover both leaf surfaces and reapply at intervals (10–14 days) during wet weather. Cultural controls are critical: use tolerant planting material, space plants adequately (8×8–12×12 ft) to reduce humidity, and avoid overhead watering. Keep the field clean by removing dead leaves. Pestalotiopsis leaf spot is generally not as destructive as Sigatoka, but high disease levels can stunt leaf growth; thus, routine monitoring and sanitation are advised.

## banana___sigatoka

Sigatoka leaf spot (yellow or black Sigatoka) is a serious banana disease caused by fungi (*Mycosphaerella* spp./*Pseudocercospora* spp.). Early symptoms are numerous small chlorotic (yellow) specks on the oldest leaves, which enlarge into cigar-shaped or elliptical brown-black lesions with a yellow halo. These lesions often coalesce, causing large necrotic areas that rapidly turn the leaf yellow and then black, leading to defoliation. High humidity and warm temperature accelerate spread.

Management is urgent once lesions appear. Remove the most severely infected leaves immediately and burn them. When about one-third of leaves (the “fourth leaf”) are infected, begin fungicide sprays and repeat every 10–14 days during rainy periods. A common regime is to alternate a protectant contact fungicide with a systemic: for example, spray chlorothalonil (500 g/L SC at 30 ml per 10 L) or Mancozeb 75% WG (20 g/10 L) on one pass, then propiconazole or tebuconazole (250 g/L at 3.5 ml/10 L) on the next. Thorough coverage of the entire canopy is critical. For prevention, use tolerant cultivars (avoid highly susceptible local varieties) and maintain wide spacing (8–12 ft). Use drip irrigation rather than sprinklers to keep leaves dry, and balance nutrients (avoid excess nitrogen). Practice good sanitation: remove dead hanging leaves and control weeds. If uncontrolled, Sigatoka can defoliate plants within weeks, so rapid treatment is essential to protect fruit yield and plant health.
