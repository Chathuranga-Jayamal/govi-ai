# chilli___anthracnose  
**Crop affected:** Chilli (Capsicum annuum). **Symptoms:** Anthracnose is a fungal disease (Colletotrichum spp.) that causes small, round, sunken brown lesions on pods and sometimes on leaves or stems.  These spots often have dark concentric rings of spores and can coalesce to rot the fruit.  Infected flowers and young fruits may fall prematurely, and severe infections can cause tip die-back and whole-plant wilting. **Cause:** The disease is caused by Colletotrichum fungi that thrive in warm, humid conditions.  Spores spread by rain splash, wind and infected seeds. **Treatment:** Remove and destroy affected plant parts and fallen debris to reduce inoculum.  Chemical control involves foliar sprays of broad-spectrum fungicides.  For example, apply Mancozeb (2.5–3 g/L water) or Copper Oxychloride (2.5–3 g/L) as soon as spots appear, repeating every 7–10 days during wet weather.  Biocontrol can help: Trichoderma-based products or Pseudomonas fluorescens soil drenches can suppress soil-borne inoculum.  Also plant healthy, disease-free seedlings and use cultivars with resistance if available. **Prevention:** Use certified disease-free seed and resistant varieties.  Practice crop rotation with non-hosts for 2–3 years and avoid planting chillies after tomatoes or peppers.  Improve field drainage and plant spacing to reduce humidity.  Always remove and burn or deeply bury crop residues at season’s end. **Severity/Urgency:** Anthracnose can rapidly spread in humid weather and rot fruits, leading to significant yield loss.  An outbreak should be treated promptly – left unchecked, it can destroy pods and reduce the next season’s seed production.  

# chilli___damping_off  
**Crop affected:** Chilli seedlings (Capsicum annuum). **Symptoms:** Damping-off shows up in nurseries or plant beds. Pre-emergence damping-off causes seeds or seedlings to rot before breaking soil, so plants fail to emerge. Post-emergence damping-off causes thin, water-soaked stems at the soil line, after which the seedling wilts and collapses.  Affected tissue may become dark and mushy, and seedlings often die in scattered patches. **Cause:** This disease is caused by soil-borne fungi such as Pythium, Fusarium, Rhizoctonia and Sclerotium, which thrive in overly wet, poorly drained soils.  Cool, waterlogged conditions and high moisture promote these pathogens. **Treatment:** Immediately remove and destroy infected seedlings to stop spread. Apply a fungicide drench in the nursery soil: for example, use metalaxyl (Mancozeb + Metalaxyl) or Trichoderma viride (beneficial fungus) as a seed treatment or soil drench according to label rates.  Treat seeds before planting (seed dressing) with Thiram or Captan (e.g. 2–3 g per kg of seed) to protect germinating seeds. Avoid overwatering.  Soil solarization (covering warm moist soil with clear plastic for 4–6 weeks before sowing) can also kill pathogens.  Maintain high sanitation: use pasteurized or sterilized potting mix if possible. **Prevention:** Sow chillies in well-drained raised beds or containers. Sterilize nursery beds by solarization or light burning before sowing. Do not grow another Solanaceae crop (tomato, eggplant, chilli) in the same bed for several seasons. Space seeds thinly (e.g. 40 g seed per 3×1 m bed) to avoid overcrowding and ensure good airflow. Avoid excess moisture – water only as needed and mulch lightly to conserve moisture without pooling. **Severity/Urgency:** Damping-off can kill seedlings very quickly – whole nursery beds can be lost within days. Attention must be immediate: if untreated, nearly 100% of young plants can succumb, so preventive action and early treatment are essential.

# chilli___leaf_curl_virus  
**Crop affected:** Chilli (Capsicum annuum) and other solanaceous crops. **Symptoms:** Infected plants develop upward curling, crinkling leaves and stunted growth.  Leaves become yellowish with distorted, small leaflets and shortened internodes.  Infected branches often produce many spindly shoots (“bunchy top” appearance) and bear few, small pods.  Fruits on diseased plants are small, often malformed, and may also exhibit yellowing. **Cause:** This is a viral disease spread by whiteflies (Bemisia tabaci).  The virus replicates systemically, and high whitefly populations in hot seasons accelerate disease spread. Contributing factors include planting during peak whitefly pressure and presence of nearby virus hosts (weeds, volunteer peppers). **Treatment:** There is no cure for the virus itself. Infected plants should be uprooted and destroyed at once to prevent spread. To control whiteflies, use systemic insecticides judiciously (e.g. apply Imidacloprid 5 ml per 10 L of water as a root drench at planting) and repeat applications on a 10–14 day schedule if whitefly pressure is high. Organic options include spraying neem oil (1–2%) or insecticidal soap on underside of leaves every 7–10 days. Yellow sticky traps can help catch adult whiteflies. Treat nearby alternate hosts (e.g. prickly lettuce, vine weeds) to reduce reservoirs of infection. **Prevention:** Use virus-free transplants and start early in the season before whitefly populations build. Establish windbreaks or intercrops (like maize) to slow flying insects. Rotate chilli with non-host crops for several years. Keep fields weed-free and remove any volunteer solanaceous plants that could harbour the virus. **Severity/Urgency:** Leaf curl virus severely reduces yield and quality; plants often fail to set marketable fruit. Because it spreads rapidly via whitefly, symptoms seen in young plants indicate an urgent need to destroy infected plants and implement vector control immediately.

# chilli___leaf_spot  
**Crop affected:** Chilli (Capsicum annuum). **Symptoms:** Leaf spot (frog-eye) caused by Cercospora capsici appears first as small round spots on older leaves. The lesions are brown with gray centers and a yellow halo, resembling “frog eyes”.  As infection progresses, spots enlarge and may coalesce.  Affected leaves turn yellow and wither, often curling inward before eventually dropping off.  Heavy leaf drop can occur in humid conditions. In severe cases, the infection may also spread to leaf stalks or fruit stems. **Cause:** This fungal disease is favored by high humidity and moderate temperatures (around 20–25°C). Cercospora fungi survive on infested crop debris and are spread by rain splash and wind. Over-fertilization with nitrogen can increase foliage and humidity in the canopy, worsening disease. **Treatment:** Remove fallen infected leaves and destroy them (burn or bury) to reduce spore load. Foliar sprays of broad-spectrum fungicides will reduce the disease: for example, apply Mancozeb or Chlorothalonil at recommended rates (typically 2–2.5 g/L water) every 7–10 days during wet weather. Organic options include Bordeaux mixture (copper sulfate + lime) or a neem-based fungicide spray at label rates. Ensure thorough coverage of leaf undersides. Maintain even soil fertility: apply organic compost or well-balanced NPK fertilizer as recommended. **Prevention:** Keep the crop well-spaced and weed-free to promote air flow and lower leaf wetness. Avoid overhead irrigation at night or when heavy dew is likely. Practice crop rotation (do not plant chillies or tomatoes in the same spot two years running). Improve soil drainage if waterlogging is an issue. Apply sufficient organic matter or mulches to encourage healthy foliage. **Severity/Urgency:** Cercospora leaf spot usually develops slowly but can become serious in sustained humid weather. Monitor regularly; if more than a few spots appear on many leaves, start treatment quickly to prevent rapid defoliation, which can reduce yields.

# chilli___veinal_mottle_virus  
**Crop affected:** Chilli (Capsicum annuum) and other pepper varieties. **Symptoms:** Veinal mottle virus (a Potyvirus) causes mottling and mosaic patterns on leaves. Early symptoms include light and dark green blotches or rings and interveinal chlorosis (yellowing) on new leaves. As the disease advances, leaves often distort or crinkle and develop green or yellow banding along the veins. Fruit on infected plants can be mottled or distorted, with pale or green stripes, and plants infected as seedlings may be stunted. **Cause:** Chilli veinal mottle virus (ChiVMV) is transmitted by aphids in a non-persistent manner. It is not seed-transmitted. Plants become infected when aphids feed briefly on a diseased plant and then move to healthy plants. The virus can also enter through mechanical damage. **Treatment:** There is no cure for infected plants. Infected plants should be immediately uprooted and destroyed to prevent aphid transmission. Disinfect tools between fields (using bleach solution) to avoid mechanical spread. Use insecticidal control targeting aphids: for example, apply neem oil or horticultural oil regularly, and consider a systemic insecticide (like Acetamiprid or Imidacloprid) if aphid populations are high, following label directions. Use yellow sticky traps and reflective plastic mulches to reduce aphid landing. Also treat nearby weeds and volunteer peppers that may harbor the virus. **Prevention:** Start with certified virus-free seedlings. Plant early in the season before aphids peak. Deploy virus-resistant or tolerant varieties if available. Remove and destroy any plants showing symptoms as soon as they appear. Avoid planting peppers next to known virus hosts (weeds or solanaceous crops) and use intercropping (e.g. maize) as a physical barrier. Maintain good field sanitation. **Severity/Urgency:** Chilli veinal mottle virus can greatly reduce yield and fruit quality. Once infection is spotted, acting immediately to eliminate the source and control aphid vectors is crucial to prevent an epidemic.

# chilli___whitefly  
**Crop affected:** Chilli (Capsicum annuum) and other vegetable crops. **Symptoms:** Heavy whitefly infestations cause a sticky residue (honeydew) on leaves and fruit, often leading to sooty mold (black fungus) growth on the surface. Infested plants may show yellowing leaves, leaf drop, and poor vigor. You can see tiny whitish insects clustering on the undersides of young leaves. Whitefly feeding causes stippling of leaves and can transmit viruses (such as leaf curl and yellow viruses), further stunting plants. **Cause:** The whitefly (Bemisia tabaci) is a sap-sucking insect that thrives in hot, dry conditions with some humidity. Dense plantings, lack of natural predators, or continuous cropping of host plants lead to outbreaks. Whiteflies can migrate from nearby fields or weeds to new chilli plantings. **Treatment:** Quick action is needed if whitefly numbers rise. Use insecticidal soaps or oils (e.g. neem oil at 1–2% concentration) and spray both upper and lower leaf surfaces every 7–10 days. For severe infestations, apply a systemic insecticide at planting or transplanting (e.g. Imidacloprid 5 ml per 10 L as a root drench) or foliar spray (always follow local guidelines). Biological control is possible: release natural enemies like ladybird beetles or predatory mites (e.g. Amblyseius swirskii has been shown to reduce whitefly in chilli). Yellow sticky traps placed in the crop can help catch flying adults. Remove heavily infested leaves and flush fields with strong water jets if possible. **Prevention:** Keep field edges free of weeds that host whiteflies, and destroy old crop debris after harvest. Introduce trap crops (e.g. marigolds or reflectively mulched beds) before planting chillies to intercept incoming whiteflies. Rotate away from perennial hosts and use crop-free periods. Use slow-release fertilizer granules rather than excessive nitrogen to avoid lush growth that attracts whiteflies. Plant chillies early or in cooler periods, as whiteflies multiply faster in very warm weather. **Severity/Urgency:** Whitefly populations can explode quickly and transmit viruses, so immediate control is vital. If numbers are left unchecked, they can severely weaken the crop and carry viruses that will ruin plants. Therefore, if lots of whiteflies are seen, treat and manage them promptly.

# chilli___yellowish  
**Crop affected:** Chilli (Capsicum annuum). **Symptoms:** General yellowing (chlorosis) of leaves is noted. Lower or older leaves may turn pale yellow, often starting at the tips or between veins, while new leaves stay greener. The overall plant may look “yellowish” and less vigorous, sometimes with slight leaf curling or drop. In mild cases, only a few leaves are pale; in severe cases, the entire canopy takes on a yellow cast. **Cause:** This symptom is often due to nutritional or environmental stress rather than a single pathogen. Common causes include nitrogen deficiency (causing older leaves to yellow), magnesium or iron deficiency (interveinal chlorosis), waterlogging or poor drainage (root stress), or even early stages of viral infection. Over-watering or compacted soil can also cause chlorosis by suffocating roots. Occasionally, mild virus infections (like mild mosaic viruses) can cause diffuse yellowing. **Treatment:** First, correct any manageable deficiency: if nitrogen-deficient (uniform yellowing of oldest leaves), side-dress with a balanced NPK fertilizer or apply urea (around 2–3 g/L as a foliar spray) to green up the plants. If iron deficiency is suspected (new leaves yellow with green veins), apply a foliar spray of chelated iron. Ensure soil pH is neutral (pH ~6) so nutrients are available. Improve drainage if waterlogging: create raised beds or furrows to remove excess moisture. Check for and eliminate any pests (thrips or aphids can cause blotchy yellowing). For mild viral causes, roguing (removing) badly yellowed plants is best, as no cure exists. **Prevention:** Regularly apply well-rotted compost or manure to maintain soil fertility and structure. Conduct a soil test before planting to balance nutrients (especially N, Fe, Mg). Avoid excessive watering; water in the morning and ensure free drainage. Use mulch to conserve moisture but avoid water-puddling at the base. Rotate chilli crops with other families (legumes, cereals) to prevent soil nutrient depletion. Monitor and promptly treat any insect pests that can transmit disease or stress the plants. **Severity/Urgency:** Generally, yellowing due to deficiency is a gradual problem; it should be corrected within a week or two by fertilization to avoid yield loss. It is not an immediate emergency like blight, but left unchecked it can reduce flowering and fruit set. If yellowing is severe and spreads quickly, reevaluate soil conditions or possible disease – addressing the cause within days to a week will prevent major impacts.

# potato___early_blight  
**Crop affected:** Potato (Solanum tuberosum). **Symptoms:** Early blight presents as small brown lesions on older foliage, usually near the bottom of the plant. These lesions expand into larger, circular spots with concentric rings, giving a “target” or bull’s-eye appearance. The spots often have a dark margin and yellow halo. As the disease advances, leaves turn yellow and die from the bottom up. In humid, warm weather, lesions may also form on stems or petioles. Tubers can develop shallow dark, corky spots, but leaf symptoms are most common. **Cause:** This disease is caused by the fungus Alternaria solani. It survives in infected debris and spreads via windblown or splashing spores. Dense canopies, high humidity, and nitrogen deficiency favor infection. It often appears mid to late season, especially if plants are stressed or when potato plants begin to senesce. **Treatment:** Start fungicide sprays when spots first appear or at flowering if the disease is known in the area. Common chemical options include protective fungicides like Mancozeb (at label rate, e.g. 2.5 g/L) or more systemic options such as Propiconazole. Apply sprays thoroughly, covering foliage, and repeat every 7–10 days as long as weather is warm and humid. Remove and burn tuber volunteers or nearby solanaceous weeds to reduce inoculum. In a nursery or seed system, treat seed tubers by dusting with Mancozeb before planting. **Prevention:** Rotate fields (don’t plant potatoes or tomatoes in the same ground for 2–3 years) to reduce soil-borne inoculum. Plant resistant or late-maturing varieties if available. Maintain proper plant nutrition with enough nitrogen to keep foliage healthy (though not excessive N). Ensure adequate spacing and hilling to improve air flow and reduce leaf wetness. Destroy potato debris after harvest to eliminate overwintering inoculum. **Severity/Urgency:** Early blight typically spreads slowly compared to late blight, so minor spots can be managed. However, if large numbers of leaves are affected (more than 20–30%), yield loss can be significant. Treating within a few days of the first noticeable lesions will keep the disease from defoliating plants before tubers mature.

# potato___late_blight  
**Crop affected:** Potato (Solanum tuberosum) and tomato. **Symptoms:** Late blight causes rapid, severe damage. On leaves, initial symptoms are pale green to water-soaked spots that quickly turn brown, dry and papery. A key sign in humid weather is white fuzzy growth (sporulation) on the underside of leaves adjacent to lesions. Infected stems and petioles show dark brown or black spots and also develop white moldy growth. On tubers, late blight produces sunken firm brown lesions under the skin; cutting such a tuber shows reddish-brown dry rot inside. Entire plants may collapse in days under cool, wet conditions. **Cause:** Phytophthora infestans, an oomycete (water mold), causes late blight. It thrives in cool (10–20°C), wet conditions. The pathogen produces wind-dispersed spores and, unlike early blight, can infect whole foliage very quickly after rains. Once established in a field, it can spread explosively. **Treatment:** Very aggressive management is needed. Immediately remove and destroy (burn or deep-bury) any infected plants or vines. Apply a systemic fungicide effective against oomycetes at first sight of symptoms or at high risk: for example, use Metalaxyl (Ridomil) or a mix of Mancozeb + a “Blight” fungicide, following label rates (e.g. 2.5 g/L). Continue sprays every 5–7 days during wet periods. Avoid handling plants when they are wet. In small plantings, copper-based fungicides can slow spread but are less effective once blight is established. **Prevention:** Use certified disease-free seed tubers and resistant varieties like ‘Carlous’ when possible. Plant early enough so vines mature before the coolest, wet weather arrives. Rotate crops and avoid planting potatoes or tomatoes after each other. Improve field drainage and avoid dense vine growth. When harvest approaches, remove foliage (haulm-kill) a couple of weeks early to reduce tuber infection. **Severity/Urgency:** Potato late blight is an emergency. It can destroy a healthy crop in days under conducive weather. Immediate, intense fungicide protection and sanitation are critical at the first sign; otherwise almost total crop loss may occur.

# corn___blight  
**Crop affected:** Maize/Corn (Zea mays). **Symptoms:** “Corn blight” usually refers to Northern Corn Leaf Blight (NCLB). It appears as long, narrow cigar-shaped lesions on leaves. Lesions are initially gray-green and later become gray-brown; they often run parallel to the leaf veins. Over time the lesions coalesce, covering large areas of leaf tissue. Infected leaves may appear tattered or even die prematurely, especially under humid conditions. Stalks and ears are usually not affected by this disease. **Cause:** The fungus Exserohilum turcicum causes NCLB, favoring warm and humid weather. Spores from infected residue or volunteer corn spread by wind and rain to infect healthy plants. High humidity and overhead irrigation (leaf wetness) promote infection. Nitrogen-rich, dense canopies also encourage the disease. **Treatment:** If blight appears, apply a fungicide spray targeting corn leaf diseases (e.g. a triazole like propiconazole or a combination product) as soon as 10–15% of plants show symptoms, especially if the weather remains humid. Follow label rates for corn. Remove and destroy severely infected lower leaves to slow spread. **Prevention:** Planting resistant or tolerant hybrids is best. Avoid continuous corn: rotate with soybean or other non-host crops for at least two years to reduce the pathogen in soil. Plow under corn stalks and debris after harvest to reduce overwintering spores. Ensure good air flow in the field by proper spacing and do not over-fertilize with nitrogen. If fields in the area had blight last year, consider an earlier planting or an extra fungicide application. **Severity/Urgency:** Northern blight spreads moderately fast under ideal conditions, but is not usually as explosive as late blight of potato. However, it can greatly reduce yield by killing foliage before tasseling. It should be managed within a week of first spotting symptoms to prevent serious losses.

# corn___common_rust  
**Crop affected:** Maize/Corn (Zea mays). **Symptoms:** Common rust causes distinct reddish-brown pustules on the upper and lower leaf surfaces. These pustules start small and round, then become elongated or oblong, often in clusters or scattered groups. Each pustule erupts through the leaf epidermis and contains powdery reddish spores. A yellow halo may form around lesions. As the disease progresses, pustules can coalesce, and infected leaves turn yellow and die prematurely. Unlike southern rust, common rust pustules can appear on both sides of leaves and typically have a cinnamon-brown color. **Cause:** Puccinia sorghi fungus causes common rust. It requires warm, humid conditions with dew for infection to occur (optimal ~16–25°C). The fungus overwinters on alternate hosts (certain Oxalis weeds) and spreads by windborne spores. Wet weather greatly increases spore spread and infection rate. **Treatment:** Destroy crop residues and alternate hosts near the field to reduce initial spores. Fungicides can be used when disease is expected to reach economic levels (e.g. when most leaves have pustules). Effective products include a mix of a broad-spectrum fungicide such as Mancozeb or Carbendazim plus a systemic like Azoxystrobin or Propiconazole. For example, Carbendazim+Mancozeb at 300–400 g/acre or Azoxystrobin+Difenoconazole at 150–200 ml/acre has been recommended. Spray thoroughly, coating both sides of leaves. In small plots or gardens, one can also remove heavily infected leaves and rely on natural drying (rust often slows down if hot, dry weather returns). **Prevention:** Use disease-resistant hybrids if available. Maintain clean field borders (remove Oxalis spp.). Rotate with non-host crops to break the rust cycle. Monitor weather: if prolonged dew or rain occurs during warm weather, be ready to spray. **Severity/Urgency:** Common rust usually develops more slowly than gray leaf spot or blight. It rarely kills plants outright, but heavy rust can reduce photosynthesis and yield (often by a few percent). Fungicide application is typically recommended only if rust symptoms are widespread (roughly 80% of plants with pustules) before or during flowering; otherwise it can be considered low priority.

# corn___gray_leaf_spot  
**Crop affected:** Maize/Corn (Zea mays). **Symptoms:** Gray leaf spot causes small, rectangular tan-to-gray lesions that run between the leaf veins. Early lesions are long and narrow, often with a chlorotic halo. As the disease progresses, lesions expand and take on a blocky shape limited by veins. Eventually the lesions turn gray-green to ashy gray with age. Under severe infection, many lesions merge, creating large dead areas of leaf tissue. Unlike blight, gray leaf spot rarely spreads to the stalk or tassels, remaining confined to leaves. **Cause:** Cercospora zeae-maydis fungus causes gray leaf spot. It survives in old corn debris and spreads via splashing rain. Warm (above 27°C) and humid weather favors the disease. Reduced-till or no-till fields, continuous corn, and fields with high residue are at higher risk. Dense canopies with high humidity (due to heavy nitrogen use) further encourage infection. **Treatment:** Manage outbreaks with fungicide if lesion levels are high during grain fill. Effective sprays include strobilurin or triazole fungicides (for example, Azoxystrobin or Pyraclostrobin) applied at silking if early symptoms are seen and weather conditions remain favorable. Follow label instructions; two applications 10–14 days apart are often used in heavy disease conditions. Crop scouting is key: spray if lesions begin appearing on upper leaves before tassel. **Prevention:** Rotate with soybean or other non-host crops for at least one year. Bury or remove corn residue (e.g. plowing) to reduce overwintering fungus. Use resistant hybrids where available. Adjust planting density to improve airflow and avoid planting corn after corn more than once. Apply only recommended nitrogen and consider tillage or cover crops to help decompose corn stalks. **Severity/Urgency:** Gray leaf spot can severely reduce yield if it occurs early and progresses into the critical grain-fill period. If spotted early in the season on lower leaves, immediate action (rotation or residue management) is important. If detected late, fungicide control is most effective before significant leaf area is lost; once most leaves are brown, treatment will not save yield.
