# coconut___bud_root_dropping  
**Crop:** Coconut. **Symptoms:** Young coconut suckers or emerging crowns may wilt suddenly and fall off. The spear leaf often bends or dries before opening, and small nuts or inflorescences may abort and drop. At the base of the bud one may see browning or soft tissue; roots of the young plant may detach or rot, and nearby growing points decay. A foul or fermented smell can sometimes be noticed in severe cases. **Cause:** Although poorly understood in Sri Lanka, bud/root dropping is often associated with fungal infection of the bud or root zone (such as *Phytophthora* spp.) and is favoured by waterlogging, poor drainage and dense shade. Stress factors like extreme rain or heat, nutrient imbalances or pest damage to the stem base can also precipitate bud failure. **Treatment:** There is no cure once a bud has dropped – affected palms should be removed to prevent spread of pathogens. For early‐stage infections, splashing recommended systemic fungicides (e.g. **metalaxyl**) on the bud region and adjacent roots may help; treat by drenching the soil around the base with a fungicide solution (label rates, e.g. metalaxyl 2–3 g/litre water). Slashing open and draining any water from the stump, then applying a thick paste of Bordeaux mixture (200 g copper sulfate + 200 g lime per 25 L water) around the bud, can limit pathogen growth. Support the palm with adequate nutrition and irrigation. **Organic/cultural:** Remove and burn any dropped buds or young palms. Improve soil drainage by earthing up around bases or installing drains. Keep palms well‐fed on balanced fertiliser (NPK) to avoid stress. **Prevention:** Maintain well‐drained, slightly acidic soil and avoid injuring the palm base. Do not plant new coconuts in waterlogged areas and ensure good stand spacing so air circulates through the crowns. Regularly inspect and promptly remove any pest‐infested or badly bent fronds that could harbor fungi. Keep the palm mat (dead frond sheaths) trimmed to reduce hidden moisture. **Urgency:** High. Once the growing bud is infected, the palm quickly loses vigour and may die in weeks if untreated. Prompt removal and sanitation are essential to protect neighboring palms.

# coconut___bud_rot  
**Crop:** Coconut. **Symptoms:** Bud rot begins in the central growing point (spear) of the palm. The spear leaf fails to unfurl and turns yellow to brown. The spear region quickly becomes soft, water‑soaked and mushy, often with a foul odor from decaying tissue. Inner leaflets yellow and droop; in advanced cases the entire crown collapses. Young fronds may wither before full expansion, and fruit/stem rot may follow. **Cause:** Bud rot is caused by the water‑mold fungus *Phytophthora palmivora*, which thrives in wet, poorly drained soils. It infects the bud, where humid, stagnant conditions allow the fungus to proliferate. Dense planting (poor ventilation), continuous shaded undergrowth, and mechanical injury to the crown favor the disease. Heavy rains or irrigation at the bud in monsoon season often trigger outbreaks. **Treatment:** Any palms showing bud rot should be cut down and destroyed immediately to stop disease spread. For marginally affected older palms, some control is possible by thoroughly drenching the bud area with systemic fungicides such as metalaxyl or fosetyl‑aluminium (e.g. apply 3–5 g metalaxyl per palm in 2–3 L water around the bud every 2 weeks). Commercial Bordeaux paste (1:1 copper sulfate:lime) or a 1–2% copper oxychloride paste can be packed into the bud (wrap a fungicide‑soaked coir hessian sack in the axil of the unopened leaf) and changed monthly to kill the fungus. Spray or spot‑treat the surrounding canopy with a registered copper fungicide at label rates. Avoid sugar‑based systemic drenches, which may worsen rot. **Organic/cultural:** Remove dead frond mats around the crown (moist mats harbor the pathogen). Ensure adequate sunlight reaches palms. Destroy all severely infected tissues. In nurseries, dip seedlings in 1% copper sulfate solution before planting. **Prevention:** Plant on raised beds or well-drained sites and avoid overwatering during monsoons. Maintain 3–5 m spacing so palms are not shaded. Treat mother palms with preventive triazole fungicide (e.g. propiconazole) during high‑risk seasons. Use tolerant varieties where available. Regularly inspect for early discoloration of the spear leaf. **Urgency:** Very high. Bud rot progresses quickly; untreated palms usually die within weeks. Rapid action is needed to remove infection and protect nearby trees.

# coconut___cci_caterpillars (Coconut Caterpillar Infestation)  
**Crop:** Coconut. **Symptoms:** Infested palms show shredded and frayed leaves, especially the lower fronds. Caterpillars of the black-headed coconut caterpillar (*Opisina arenosella*) spin white silken webs on leaf undersides with frass (droppings). Characteristic “scorched” patches appear as webs accumulate over leaflet surfaces. You may see greenish‐brown caterpillars with dark heads moving among webbing or pupae in silken cocoons inside the fronds. In severe cases, all but the youngest 3–4 leaves turn brown and crispy, giving the plantation a burnt-out look. **Cause:** This is an insect pest, not a disease. Outbreaks occur most often in dry, hot weather (typically March–May) or in plantations deprived of water. Dense leaf axils and overgrown palms allow caterpillars to hide. Poor field hygiene (leaf litter, fallen fronds) and lack of natural enemies (parasitic wasps, parasitoids) contribute. **Treatment:** Early detection is key. At first sight of webbing, cut the affected fronds flush to the trunk and burn them. For chemical control, inject or pour *carbaryl* or a pyrethroid into the leaf axil of the youngest green fronds (mix carbaryl 20 g or permethrin 2 mL per 1 L water; one palm needs ~2–3 L solution) and plug the holes in the sheath so the liquid drains slowly to the crown. Repeat every 7–14 days until no live caterpillars remain. Bacillus thuringiensis sprays (at ~2 g/L) can be used as an organic alternative on younger palms. Ensure good coverage of webbed areas. **Organic/cultural:** Encourage parasitic wasps (e.g. *Brachymeria* spp.) and birds by intercropping and leaving some undisturbed habitats. Release fly or wasp parasitoids if available. Drop petroleum “mothballs” (naphthalene) or neem cake pieces into the unopened sheaths of each youngest leaf; these fumigants can kill early instars. Pheromone traps exist for monitoring moths. Avoid over-application of high nitrogen fertilizers, which can favor outbreaks. **Prevention:** Maintain palm vigor with regular fertilisation and irrigation, making trees less attractive to caterpillars. Trim older brown fronds and broom overgrowth so air flows freely. Monitor fortnightly and destroy any early‐infested leaves. Encourage bats and beneficial insects by planting flowering border crops. **Urgency:** Medium. Early infestations can spread widely, but affected palms rarely die outright. Regular weekly inspections are needed; heavy defoliation over several months will seriously reduce yields if left unchecked.

# coconut___cci_leaflets (Coconut Caterpillar Infestation — leaflet damage)  
**Crop:** Coconut. **Symptoms:** Individual leaflets on lower leaves show damage: ragged holes, notches or completely eaten areas. The undersides have silken webs and frass. Dead patches (“burnt” appearance) appear on the fronds as caterpillars skeletonize the leaflets. You may see threads of white silk smeared with dark droppings. The older leaves may die back, leaving only a few green ones at the top of the palm. Inspect undersides of leaflets for green caterpillars up to 3 cm long with dark head capsules. **Cause:** Same *Opisina* caterpillar as above; larvae feed exclusively on leaflet tissue. Outbreaks intensify when palms are weakened by drought or poor nutrition. Dense leaf clusters provide shelter, while lack of natural enemies and absence of cultural control let populations build. **Treatment:** Control measures are the same as for general caterpillar infestation. Cut and burn any heavily webbed leaves (check before burning that no live larvae escape). Spray targeted insecticide into rolled‐up leaflet axils: for example, apply 1% *carbaryl* (20 g/l) or a pyrethroid (e.g. 0.05% cypermethrin) on affected leaf whorls, ensuring liquid penetrates the webbing. Alternatively, trunk‐inject the insecticide solution: drill a 6–8 mm hole 1–2 m above ground, insert a 15 cm piece of bamboo, pour 1–2 L of solution through a funnel every 2–3 weeks. Monitor and retreat as needed. **Organic/cultural:** The same cultural steps apply: remove infested leaf tissue early, use neem‐based sprays (5% neem oil) on webs, and apply Bt as a last resort (carefully target larvae to avoid killing parasitoids). Use sticky bands on trunks to trap migrating caterpillars moving between leaves. Introduce available natural enemies (via graftings or conservation) like the wasp *Goniozus nephantidis*. **Prevention:** Maintain healthy palms with balanced fertiliser (especially potash), as vigor reduces attack. Keep plantations clean: burn prunings and dry leaves to remove breeding sites. Stagger planting ages and interplant with non‐host cover crops to break the caterpillar cycle. **Urgency:** Low to medium. Defoliation is unsightly and lowers yield over time, but a few normal fronds usually remain. Routine checks and month‑ly control should prevent serious loss.

# coconut___gray_leaf_spot  
**Crop:** Coconut. **Symptoms:** Initially, small grayish‑white or tan spots appear on older coconut leaflets, often near midrib or edges. Each lesion is ringed by a brown margin. As spots enlarge they coalesce into irregular pale patches. In heavy infections the leaf tissue between spots blights and dries, giving the frond a “speckled” or blighted look. Severely diseased leaves may yellow and die prematurely. Inner whorls remain green longer, but progressive spotting can slowly weaken the palm. **Cause:** Gray leaf spot is a fungal disease, usually caused by *Pestalotiopsis* species or related fungi on coconuts. High humidity and poor air circulation (e.g. dense plantation, high rainfall with little wind) favor this pathogen. Spores splash from wet, injured leaf surfaces, so overcrowding and continuously moist leaf axils contribute. **Treatment:** Copper-based fungicides can suppress gray leaf spot. Spray a 1% copper oxychloride or Bordeaux mixture solution on the affected canopy, covering both leaf surfaces, every 10–14 days during wet weather. Systemic fungicides (e.g. azoxystrobin at label rate) may be applied if infection is severe, following the manufacturer’s dose. Carefully prune out and destroy the most heavily blighted fronds to remove inoculum. **Organic/cultural:** Prune off old dead fronds regularly, so new fronds have light and air. Avoid overhead irrigation that keeps leaves wet. Compost fallen infected leaves thoroughly or burn them. Encourage good canopy ventilation by spacing trees and thinning adjacent vegetation. **Prevention:** Plant varieties reported as tolerant (if known in Sri Lanka). Apply preventive copper sprays during monsoon or when overhead irrigation cannot be avoided. Maintain overall palm health with adequate nutrition, as vigorous trees recover faster. **Urgency:** Low. Gray leaf spot is typically minor on coconut; it rarely kills palms. Left unchecked it will slowly reduce photosynthetic area and may stress the palm over years. Routine monitoring and occasional fungicide treatments prevent outbreaks.

# coconut___leaf_rot  
**Crop:** Coconut. **Symptoms:** Leaf rot usually shows as dark brown or black lesions on coconut leaflets and fronds. The spots often begin on the inner whorl or older leaves. They may have chlorotic halos or appear sunken and moist. Severe infections cause full areas of lamina to turn rotten, withered and brittle. The disease may first manifest as dark streaks along leaflet veins, then spread across the leaf. Unlike Weligama wilt, entire fronds do not typically collapse, but leaf area is lost. Blackening around leaf bases may be seen if a secondary stem rot occurs. **Cause:** “Leaf rot” in Sri Lankan coconuts is a complex of fungal pathogens (often *Lasiodiplodia (Botryodiplodia) theobromae*, *Pestalotiopsis* spp. and others) acting on palms already weakened by root wilt or environmental stress. It is favored by high humidity, stagnant air, and poor drainage. Water spores or windspread spores infect leaves, especially those injured by mechanical damage or insect feeding. Nutrient imbalances (especially low nitrogen) can predispose palms. **Treatment:** Once leaf rot appears, apply protective sprays. Use a 1% Bordeaux mixture or copper oxychloride on the whole crown to kill fungi; repeat every 3–4 weeks during wet conditions. Systemic protectants like mancozeb (0.25%) or propiconazole (0.1%) can also be sprayed on foliage. Remove and destroy badly rotted fronds to reduce spread; avoid splashing debris onto healthy fronds. Improve drainage around palms to limit moisture. **Organic/cultural:** Improve field drainage (dikes, ridges) to prevent waterlogging. Ensure proper nutrition, especially nitrogen and potassium, to keep palms vigorous. Apply gypsum or organic matter to acidic soils to improve structure. **Prevention:** Regularly remove dead leaflets and trash from the crown. Do not allow nematodes (root knots) to weaken palms. A yearly copper spray prophylactically (e.g. 0.75% Bordeaux) is often recommended in high‐rainfall zones. Keep adjacent crops trimmed so air passes through. **Urgency:** Low to medium. Leaf rot is rarely fatal on its own but indicates underlying stress. Untreated, it can lead to gradual decline in yield and palm vigor. Prompt care after first lesions will prevent severe infection.

# coconut___stem_bleeding  
**Crop:** Coconut. **Symptoms:** Stem bleeding manifests as dark reddish‑brown exudate oozing from cracks or wounds on the coconut trunk. A wet stain or stream of gummy fluid runs down the trunk surface. On wiping, a sticky, foul‑smelling liquid and reddish fungal strands are evident. Infected bark often becomes sunken, and a white mycelial ring may form inside the wood under the wound. Over time, the palm’s crown may weaken, nuts drop prematurely, and the palm tapers (thin leaves, stunted growth). Brown discoloration often extends from the exudation point into the trunk. **Cause:** A soil fungus (*Thielaviopsis paradoxa* or *Ceratocystis paradoxa*) enters through injuries at the trunk base or browsed wounds (stem cracking, root wounds, termite damage). It thrives in poorly drained, low‑oxygen soils. Once inside, it rots the internal tissues and forces fluid out through cracks. High humidity and continuous wet soil increase incidence. Lignite‑rich acid soils are particularly prone. **Treatment:** Scrape away all discolored, rotted bark around the bleeding lesion until clean wood is reached. Paint the cleaned wound generously with Bordeaux paste (200 g copper sulfate + 200 g lime per 25 L water). Refill the cleaned cavity with a mixture of Bordeaux paste and 3% urea (to burn out remaining hyphae). Cover with tape or bag for 1–2 weeks. Repeat this process every month until exudation stops. In Sri Lanka, successful control has been reported using 3% urea + 1% Bordeaux paste. Ensure good drainage and reduce water around the base to lessen pathogen survival. **Organic/cultural:** Improve base drainage by slight earthing up or ridging. Remove excess mulch from the trunk and ensure no prolonged flooding. Avoid mechanical damage to the trunk (by tools or animals). **Prevention:** Prevent injuries: stake young palms to prevent lodging, avoid cutting fruit husks that gouge the trunk, and fence out large animals. Maintain soil health: high organic matter and moderate moisture discourage the fungus. Rotate or fallow badly infested palms. **Urgency:** Medium. Bleeding indicates serious internal decay; an infected palm will likely die within 1–2 years if untreated. Early intervention by cleaning and paste application can arrest the disease. Without treatment, the fungus can spread to roots and adjacent palms.

# coconut___wclwd_drying_of_leaflets (Weligama Coconut Leaf Wilt Disease)  
 *Figure: Adjacent coconut palms; the right palm exhibits severe Weligama Leaf Wilt symptoms (leaflet necrosis and drying) compared to the healthy green palm on the left*. **Crop:** Coconut. **Symptoms:** In this stage of WCLWD, leaflet tips and margins turn brown, dry and shrivel while the rest of the leaflet may stay green. These necrotic patches progress inward over weeks, so that leaflets appear burnt or torn. Affected fronds lose their stiffness – they hang limply. Eventually entire leaflets can dry out and fall, leaving thin leaves. Close inspection often shows that inner fronds (mid whorl) remain green briefly, but outer/older ones scorch from the edges inward. **Cause:** Weligama Coconut Leaf Wilt is caused by a phytoplasma (a bacteria-like pathogen) transmitted by leafhoppers or planthoppers. It resembles lethal yellowing of palms. The phytoplasma blocks vascular flow, causing leaflet dehydration (drying). Favoring conditions are not fully known but the disease is endemic in south coast plantations. **Treatment:** There is no chemical cure. Some growers attempt trunk injection of oxytetracycline (250–500 mg per palm, every 3–4 months) to slow symptoms, but field success is limited. The main control is phytosanitation: as soon as leaflet necrosis is observed, the whole palm should be cut down (to prevent it becoming a source of inoculum) and burned. If nearby palms show early signs, spray the stump cut with 3% cupric oxide (wettable sulphur) powder or Bordeaux paste. Maintain field hygiene and vector control: apply a foliar insecticide (e.g. imidacloprid at 1 mL/L) on surrounding palms to kill leafhoppers. **Organic/cultural:** Promptly remove dying palms. Do not compost infected palms (burn them instead). Encourage predator and parasitoid insects by avoiding broad‑spectrum sprays. Isolation of new plantings (quarantine) from known outbreak zones is recommended. **Prevention:** Use resistant/tolerant coconut varieties where identified. Strictly follow disease‐free certification when acquiring seednuts. Avoid planting any palm species (including ornamental cycads) in or near wilt zones, since they may harbor the pathogen. Apply preventive foliar insecticide spray (acephate 0.5%) in high‑risk areas when leafhoppers are present. **Urgency:** Very high. Once leaflets start drying, the palm is doomed; death follows in months to a year. Immediate removal of infected palms is critical to protect healthy ones.

# coconut___wclwd_flaccidity (Weligama Coconut Leaf Wilt Disease)  
**Crop:** Coconut. **Symptoms:** The most striking symptom is loss of turgor in the fronds: leaves and leaflets become flaccid and hang down. The crown looks wilted or limp even though roots may be fine. Unlike drought, moisture is usually adequate. Often flaccidity appears along with marginal necrosis (as above) or intense yellowing (see next entry). Early on, affected fronds droop while older fronds may remain up briefly. Trees may still have a full set of green leaves, but these are limp rather than erect and often develop dry tips within weeks. **Cause:** Same phytoplasma (WCLWD) as above. The pathogen disrupts water and nutrient flow to the leaves, causing them to go limp. It is spread by plant‐sucking vectors (leafhoppers) or via contaminated tools. High rainfall areas with dense vegetation may favor vector populations, but even isolated palms get infected if in the endemic zone. **Treatment:** No curative treatment exists. Trunk injection with antibiotics (tetracycline/oxytetracycline) may temporarily improve vigor, but this is expensive and not widely practiced in Sri Lanka. Instead, focus on containment: cut down any palm with noticeable flaccidity, especially if combined with leaflet drying or yellowing, and burn it. Treat the stump with Bordeaux paste. Spray neighboring palms with systemic insecticides (e.g. imidacloprid 0.5%, as a soil drench) to control probable leafhopper vectors. **Organic/cultural:** Maintain a buffer zone: do not allow other palms in the area. Remove alternate hosts (some bamboo or banana can harbor related phytoplasmas). Clean tools with disinfectant between palms. **Prevention:** Similar to other WCLWD prevention: use seedlings from disease-free nurseries, and follow any local movement restrictions. Monitor closely during rainy season when leafhoppers are active, and apply a preventive neem or tobacco extract spray to repel insects. **Urgency:** High. Flaccid palms may temporarily carry on but ultimately collapse as the internal infection spreads. If untreated, these palms will die in months and serve as reservoirs for the pathogen. Early detection and felling are the only effective measures.

# coconut___wclwd_yellowing (Weligama Coconut Leaf Wilt Disease)  
**Crop:** Coconut. **Symptoms:** Affected palms display intense yellowing (chlorosis) of the fronds, especially the younger central leaves. Instead of the normal green, fronds turn uniformly yellow or pale. Early signs can be uneven yellowing in the mid-crown; eventually, yellow spreads to all fronds. Yellowed leaves often develop brown edges or dry patches afterward. Unlike nutritional deficiency, palms have plenty of leaves but they lose chlorophyll quickly. The crown may appear sparse as yellowed fronds wilt and die. On careful checking, flaccidity (limpness) of these yellow leaves is also common. **Cause:** This is another expression of WCLWD. The phytoplasma causes a syndrome very similar to coconut lethal yellowing or Kerala wilt – hence palms “yellow” rapidly. High humidity and wet soil increase spread of the pathogens and insect vectors. As with other WCLWD symptoms, the underlying cause is a plant pathogenic phytoplasma carried by sap-sucking insects. **Treatment:** No effective cure exists. The role of antibiotics is the same (discussed above), but not a practical large-scale solution. Remove and burn any tree showing irreversible yellowing; if only partial, severe pruning of discolored leaves may slow spread. Treat stumps with 3% cupric oxide paste. Apply systemic insecticide (neem or chemical) to nearby healthy palms as a preventive. **Organic/cultural:** Strip off yellowed fronds as soon as they appear to reduce inoculum. Maintain sanitation: disinfect pruning tools. Grow flowering legumes or marigolds under palms to support natural enemies of vectors. **Prevention:** Plant resistant varieties if known (or rootstock grafting onto resistant seedling rootstocks). Enforce phytosanitary regulations: do not transport palms or cut fronds from affected areas (Sri Lankan law prohibits movement of palms from wilt zones). Apply a preventive regimen of foliar insecticide (e.g. chlorpyrifos 0.5% spray every 2 months) to protect against potential vectors. **Urgency:** Very high. Yellowing palms go into decline and die in a matter of months. Prompt eradication of yellowing palms is essential – delaying even a few weeks can allow the insect vector to spread the phytoplasma to healthy trees.

**Sources:** Authoritative Sri Lankan and international references on coconut pests and diseases were used, including the Sri Lanka Coconut Research Institute (CRI), pest reports and academic studies. In particular, treatments are drawn from CRI extension recommendations and published disease management guides.
