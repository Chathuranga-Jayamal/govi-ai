## rice___bacterial_blight

**Crop affected:** Rice. **Symptoms:** Young seedlings show wilting and yellowing of leaves (“kresek”), often dying at the base. In older plants, look for water-soaked pale-green or orange bands on leaf blades and tips; these lesions have wavy margins and extend downwards. When squeezed, infected cut leaves may ooze yellowish bacterial liquid (a key sign distinguishing it from stem borer). **Cause:** A bacterium, *Xanthomonas oryzae* pv. *oryzae*, causes the disease. It thrives in warm (25–34°C), humid conditions with heavy rains or strong wind-driven rain, especially where high nitrogen fertiliser is used. Water-logged or poorly drained fields and excessive nitrogen also favour spread. **Treatment:** Remove and destroy badly infected plants and dry out paddy fields immediately to limit spread. If bacterial blight appears, follow the national fertilizer advice: apply urea only as per the leaf colour chart rather than blanket extra nitrogen. Spraying specific bactericides can help: for example, apply kasugamycin or streptocycline (as per local label rates) soon after symptoms first appear. Organic options include drenching seed or soil with a zinc solution (ZnSO₄ at 0.5–1%) or using antagonistic bacteria. **Prevention:** Use certified disease-free seed and resistant rice varieties where available. Ensure fields are well drained and avoid over-fertilising with nitrogen (use balanced N-P-K and organic manure). Rotate rice with non-host crops and remove volunteer grasses or weeds that can harbor bacteria. Practise good sanitation: don’t plant rice next to fields with recent blight history. **Severity/Urgency:** Bacterial blight can rapidly spread and cause heavy yield losses if unchecked. Young plants are most vulnerable (kresek can destroy seedbeds), so treat outbreaks immediately. If left unmanaged, entire fields may become blighted, severely reducing grain yield.

## rice___blast

**Crop affected:** Rice. **Symptoms:** Brownish spindle- or diamond-shaped spots with concentric rings appear on leaves; spots have reddish-brown or yellow-brown margins and grey or ashy centers. As lesions enlarge (typically 1–1.5 cm long), they may coalesce. Infected leaf nodes turn black and rot, causing “neck blast” where panicles die (“whiteheads”) before grain fills. Panicle base infection causes the panicle to break off. Severe leaf blast defoliates plants, exposing grains to sunburn. **Cause:** A fungus (*Magnaporthe oryzae*) causes blast. It infects all growth stages of rice (leaves, nodes, panicles). Cool night temperatures (17–20°C) with high humidity, fog or heavy dew, dense planting, and excessive nitrogen fertiliser favor the disease. Weak or susceptible varieties are much affected. **Treatment:** Immediately remove heavily infected plants or sections to reduce spore load. Fungicidal sprays can help if caught early. In Sri Lanka, recommended foliar sprays include tricyclazole (75% WP at 10 g in 16 L water per acre) or carbendazim (50% WP at ~10–11 g per 16 L), applied when symptoms first appear. Rotate to a different FRAC class if multiple applications are needed. Cultural measures also help: apply urea judiciously (use a leaf colour chart) and ensure weeds are removed. After harvest, burn or plow under straw to kill spores. **Prevention:** Use blast-resistant varieties (Sri Lankan lines such as Bg 403, Bw 361, etc. if available). Start with clean seed and avoid planting immediately after harvest of another paddy (to break disease cycle). Maintain balanced fertiliser – avoid overuse of nitrogen. Ensure good ventilation: space plants adequately and avoid creating a thick canopy. Keep crop debris and volunteer rice cleared, and practice 2–3-year rotations with non-rice crops. **Severity/Urgency:** Rice blast is a highly destructive disease that can cause serious yield loss and even complete crop failure if left unchecked. Control measures should be applied at the first sign of symptoms. Without treatment, blast can defoliate foliage and rot necks rapidly, especially in conducive weather.

## rice___brown_spot

**Crop affected:** Rice. **Symptoms:** Tiny, circular to oval dark brown spots appear on leaves and leaf sheaths, often starting on young plants and increasing as plants age. Spots have a dark-brown margin and lighter brown or gray center (older lesions can become large and irregular). On seedlings, dense infection causes seedling blight (sparce stands, wilting). On maturing plants, brown streaks can form on the husks of grains (spots on glumes turn black) and severely infected grains may be shrunken or pale. **Cause:** A fungus (*Cochliobolus miyabeanus*, formerly *Bipolaris oryzae*) causes brown spot. It is often seed-borne (infects seedlings and then spreads). It thrives in humid conditions and under environmental stress. It is favored by high humidity (86–100%), soil nutrient deficiencies (especially low nitrogen or potassium), drought stress, and acid or iron-toxic soils. Brown spot commonly appears when plants begin to senesce. **Treatment:** There is no cure for late-stage infection, so focus is on protecting healthy plants. Seed treatment helps: hot-water seed disinfestation (53–54°C for 10–15 min) or a seed fungicide (e.g. carbendazim or thiram at ~2–3 g per kg seed) can reduce initial infection. During growth, apply broad-spectrum foliar fungicides at the first sign of disease – for example, tricyclazole or propiconazole sprays (follow label for ~0.1–0.2% solutions) or a combination containing mancozeb or copper. Maintain soil fertility: ensure adequate potassium and balanced N, P, K to strengthen plants. Remove and burn crop residues after harvest to eliminate overwintering fungus. **Prevention:** Use certified clean seed (free from brown spot fungus) and consider resistant varieties if identified. Practice 2–3 year rotation away from rice (e.g. to legumes) and control grassy weeds, which can harbor the fungus. Improve soil health with organic matter (compost or rice husk) to reduce stress. Maintain optimal water management: avoid drought stress by irrigating adequately. Level fields properly to improve drainage and avoid waterlogging or prolonged dryness. **Severity/Urgency:** Brown spot is generally a slower-developing disease; it rarely kills plants outright. However, severe epidemics (especially on panicles) can reduce yield and grain quality. It should be managed proactively during the season to prevent serious losses, though it is less immediately urgent than blast or bacterial blight.

## rice___tungro

**Crop affected:** Rice. **Symptoms:** Infected rice plants become markedly stunted, with most leaves exhibiting a distinct yellow to orange leaf discoloration. New leaves may emerge narrow and upright (“at angles”) and exhibit interveinal yellow mottling. Older leaves turn a yellow-orange color. Tiller number is reduced and plants may bear few or no panicles. Severe tungro causes a “bushy” appearance from many short tillers, and low fertility (flowers often abort). **Cause:** Rice tungro is caused by two viruses, Rice tungro bacilliform virus (RTBV) and Rice tungro spherical virus (RTSV), spread by the green rice leafhopper (*Nephotettix virescens*). The viruses in combination produce the characteristic symptoms (RTBV causes the yellowing and stunting). Wet-season, intensively managed paddy fields favor leafhopper build-up and virus spread. **Treatment:** There is no treatment once plants are infected. Focus on controlling vectors. Spray insecticides to kill green leafhoppers early if tungro is detected – for example, apply malathion or dimethoate at label rates soon after transplanting and if leafhoppers are seen. Where available, use systemic seed treatments (e.g. imidacloprid or thiamethoxam) before sowing to reduce initial leafhopper feeding. Roguing: Uproot and destroy any infected plants as soon as they appear to reduce spread, but this is often impractical in heavy outbreaks. **Prevention:** Plant tungro-resistant or tolerant varieties (ask local extension for recommended cultivars). Use certified tungro-free seed and seedlings. Manage weeds and volunteer rice that serve as virus/leafhopper reservoirs. Synchronise planting so that fields are planted and harvested around the same time (reducing green bridge). Create trap barriers: sow field edges with tall rice to attract leafhoppers. Maintain a tomato-free crop-free period before rice planting in drought-prone areas (leafhoppers survive on alternate hosts, so a “virus-free” fallow helps). Ensure good nutrition (balanced N-P-K), as healthier plants tolerate infection better. **Severity/Urgency:** Tungro is a very serious viral disease: in heavily infested fields, it can cause complete crop failure. Yield losses are often very high if tungro spreads unchecked. Rapid vector control (and replanting, if possible) is critical once symptoms are seen. Because symptoms develop slowly over a few weeks, fields should be monitored and leafhoppers controlled as a precaution.

## tomato___bacterial_spot

**Crop affected:** Tomato. **Symptoms:** Small water-soaked flecks first appear on young leaves and stems, often along margins. These enlarge to round to angular brown lesions (2–5 mm) with a yellow halo. Severe infection causes leaf bronzing, deformation and early leaf drop. On fruit, lesions look like rough, raised brown scabs (akin to pockmarks). In warm wet weather, lesions on stems and flower stalks may appear, and cankers can girdle young shoots. **Cause:** Bacterial spot is caused by several *Xanthomonas* and *Pseudomonas* species (e.g. *Xanthomonas vesicatoria*, *P. syringae*), which survive on seed and plant debris. Warm (25–30°C), wet conditions and overhead irrigation spread the bacteria (rain splash, irrigation). Diseased seed or transplants and infested weeds/volunteers can introduce the pathogen. **Treatment:** There is no cure for infected tissue. Protect healthy plants by applying copper-based bactericides (e.g. copper oxychloride or copper hydroxide sprays) at first sign of disease; apply every 7–10 days during warm, humid weather. To improve control and prevent resistance, tank-mix copper with a protectant fungicide like mancozeb or fixed copper formulation. For organic management, copper sprays are still the main option (use an OMRI-approved copper) and ensure thorough coverage of foliage. Increase potassium nutrition (K boosts resistance). Remove heavily infected leaves or suckers and destroy them (do not compost). Seed disinfection (e.g. soak seeds in 10% bleach for 10 min, then rinse) or hot-water treatment (50°C for 25 min) can help reduce seed-borne bacteria. **Prevention:** Use certified disease-free seed or transplants. Where available, grow resistant varieties (some cultivars have partial resistance). Avoid overhead irrigation and irrigation late in the day so foliage dries quickly. Space and stake plants for good air flow. Sanitize tools between uses and avoid working in fields when foliage is wet. Remove volunteer tomato plants and nearby susceptible weeds. Rotate tomato fields with non-host crops for 2–3 years. **Severity/Urgency:** Bacterial spot spreads rapidly in warm, wet weather. Once established in the field, it is very difficult to eradicate. Heavy infections can defoliate plants and ruin fruit, so spot outbreaks should be managed promptly. Moderate urgency: protect young plants vigorously, as widespread infection during flowering can destroy the crop.

## tomato___early_blight

**Crop affected:** Tomato. **Symptoms:** Initial lesions are irregular dark brown or black spots (often with concentric rings) on the oldest leaves near the plant base. These lesions typically have a target-like “bull’s-eye” pattern: a gray center surrounded by a dark ring and a yellow halo. Infected leaves yellow between and around spots, then dry up and fall off, thinning the plant. Spots can also appear on stems and older green fruits (leading to sunken scabbed areas). **Cause:** Early blight is caused by the fungus *Alternaria solani*. It persists in soil and on plant debris. It thrives in warm (24–29°C), humid conditions, especially following heavy rain or overhead irrigation. Dense canopies or plants touching soil are more prone to infection. Over-fertilization with nitrogen can also increase susceptibility. **Treatment:** Apply a protective fungicide as soon as lesions appear and continue at 7–14 day intervals during wet weather. Effective fungicides include chlorothalonil, mancozeb or strobilurins (azoxystrobin, trifloxystrobin), or demethylation inhibitors (e.g. difenoconazole). Rotate between FRAC groups to reduce resistance. For organic control, use a Bacillus subtilis–based biofungicide or OMRI-approved copper spray. Remove affected lower leaves (especially those touching the soil) and destroy them to reduce inoculum. Ensure good fertilisation (especially calcium and potassium) to strengthen plants. **Prevention:** Start with healthy transplants and rotate tomatoes to different fields for 2–3 years. Provide adequate spacing between plants and stake or trellis vines to improve air flow and reduce humidity. Mulch around plants to prevent soil splashing onto leaves. Water in the morning so foliage dries by evening. Scouting is important; remove any infected leaves immediately to slow disease spread. Use early-maturing varieties if late season humidity is high. **Severity/Urgency:** Early blight is slow-moving but will progressively defoliate plants if unchecked. It should be managed early – fungicides must be applied preventatively, as they do not cure already diseased tissue. With timely treatment and cultural care, yield loss can be minimized. Left untreated, it can cause serious defoliation and reduced yield and quality.

## tomato___late_blight

**Crop affected:** Tomato. **Symptoms:** Small water-soaked or olive-green patches first appear on lower leaves, rapidly enlarging into brown-black blighted areas that can engulf entire leaflets. In humid weather a white-gray fuzzy mold (sporangia) appears on the underside of lesions. Leaves curl and collapse; stems may also show large brown lesions. On fruit, lesions appear as gray-brown, greasy spots, often with a water-soaked border; the flesh under these spots becomes hard and brown (leathery). **Cause:** Late blight is caused by *Phytophthora infestans*, an oomycete (water mold). It favors cool, very humid conditions (around 18–22°C, >90% RH) and spreads by wind and water. It can overwinter in volunteer tomatoes or potatoes and is an especially grave threat in tropical/subtropical wet seasons. **Treatment:** Remove and destroy infected plants immediately (do not compost). Spray fungicides preventatively before or as soon as the first lesions appear. Effective products include chlorothalonil, mancozeb, or systemic oomycete fungicides (such as metalaxyl or newer agents like mandipropamid). Apply according to label, repeating 10–14 days or as needed in rainy conditions. Ensure thorough coverage of undersides and interior canopy. Increase potassium in fertilizer (foliar K may help plant vigor). **Prevention:** Use certified disease-free seed/starts. Space plants for good air flow and avoid overhead irrigation; water early in the day. Rotate crops and avoid planting tomato or potato near each other. Install rain shelters or cover plants if rain is heavy. Remove volunteer nightshades or potato tubers. In regions with winter, consider a brief rotation that leaves fields free of Solanaceae for a few months. Plant varieties with partial resistance if available. **Severity/Urgency:** Late blight can devastate a tomato crop within days under favorable conditions. It is urgent to act at first sight of symptoms. If left untreated, entire fields can be lost. High humidity in Sri Lanka means vigilance is needed during monsoon seasons.

## tomato___leaf_mold

**Crop affected:** Tomato (especially in protected/high-humidity environments). **Symptoms:** Pale yellow irregular spots appear on the upper leaf surface (starting on oldest leaves). On the underside, a velvety olive-green mold grows below the spots. Infected leaves turn brown and die, often remaining attached to the plant. In severe cases, even blossoms and fruit can become infected: blackened flowers may drop, and fruit may develop smooth black lesions at the stem end that become sunken and leathery. Overall, infected plants are chlorotic and may collapse. **Cause:** Leaf mold is caused by the fungus *Passalora fulva* (syn. *Fulvia fulva*), which thrives when humidity stays above ~85%. It requires very humid, slow-drying conditions and is generally a problem in greenhouses or dense canopies. It is not common in open fields unless conditions are extremely wet. **Treatment:** Remove and destroy severely affected leaves or entire plants (especially seedlings) to prevent spread. Apply fungicides before infections establish: protectant sprays of mancozeb, chlorothalonil or copper hydroxide can reduce severity (apply when weather is humid and repeat per label). Organic growers can use copper hydroxide (OMRI-approved) following guidance (choose a formulation labeled for organic use). Keep greenhouse/tunnel vents open and run fans to lower humidity immediately after application. **Prevention:** Start with certified disease-free transplants. Use resistant varieties if effective ones are known (check seed catalogs for Cf gene varieties). Space plants and prune lower leaves to improve air circulation. Avoid overhead irrigation – use drip irrigation, or water at the base in the morning so leaves dry quickly. Maintain greenhouse temperature slightly above outdoor to reduce dew on leaves. After harvest, remove and destroy all debris and sanitize greenhouse walls. Ensure plants are well-fertilized (especially avoid nitrogen excess, which promotes lush canopies). **Severity/Urgency:** Leaf mold usually progresses slower than blights, but unchecked it can defoliate plants and ruin yield. It is most severe in high-humidity shelters, so humidity management is key. Control should be preventative; once many leaves are covered in mold, control is difficult. Moderate urgency: manage humidity and apply fungicide at early warning signs.

## tomato___mosaic_virus

**Crop affected:** Tomato. **Symptoms:** Leaves develop a mottled “mosaic” of light and dark green patches. Young leaves are often distorted, brittle or crinkled (“fernleaf” appearance). Infected plants are stunted with shortened internodes, often giving a bonsai-like bushy appearance. Leaves may curl upward and show interveinal yellowing. Older plants may show fruit ringspots or uneven ripening, but fruits are less commonly symptomatic. Tobacco mosaic virus (TMV) and Tomato mosaic virus (ToMV) cause these symptoms. Cucumber mosaic virus (CMV) causes a related syndrome with more leaf distortion (“shoestring” leaves) and excessive yellowing (not covered here). **Cause:** This is a viral disease (commonly TMV or ToMV). The virus spreads mechanically (on workers’ hands, tools, contaminated stakes, or via infected seed) and also via infected transplants. TMV can persist on tobacco or weeds. (CMV is transmitted by aphid vectors, but TMV/ToMV are non-vector, seed/soil-borne.) **Treatment:** There is no cure for infected plants. Remove and destroy (burn) any heavily symptomatic plants immediately to prevent spread. Disinfect tools and work clothes between fields with bleach solution. Avoid working the crop when plants are wet. After handling tobacco or other solanaceous plants, wash hands/boots before touching tomatoes. **Prevention:** Plant mosaic-resistant varieties (tomatoes with the Tm-1, Tm-2, or Tm-2² resistance genes resist TMV/ToMV). Use certified virus-free seed and transplants (even a few contaminated seedlings can spread the virus). Heat-treat seed (e.g. 70°C for 10 min) or use seed soaking protocols for TMV/ToMV to kill surface virus. Avoid planting tomatoes near tobacco crops. Control aphids to reduce CMV risk: use yellow sticky traps and insecticidal soaps early if needed. Rogue out volunteer tomatoes and control weeds (like nightshade or jimsonweed) that may harbor virus. Maintain crop hygiene and rotate crops (no tomatoes or peppers for at least two years in the affected field). **Severity/Urgency:** Tomato mosaic viruses usually cause chronic damage rather than sudden crop collapse, but they significantly reduce yield and fruit quality. Infected fields rarely recover yield in that season, so preventing spread is vital. Urgency is moderate: once plants show mosaic, remove them, and focus on sanitation and resistant plants to prevent epidemic spread.

## tomato___septoria_leaf_spot

**Crop affected:** Tomato. **Symptoms:** Small (1–2 mm) round to angular dark brown to black spots appear on lower leaves, often surrounding stomata. Each spot typically has a light tan or gray center and a dark border. As spots increase in number, leaves yellow between spots and eventually collapse. In heavy cases, large portions of older foliage are killed. Fruit are rarely affected. **Cause:** *Septoria lycopersici*, a fungus, causes septoria leaf spot. It survives on infested debris and solanaceous weeds (e.g. black nightshade). High humidity and warm (20–26°C) conditions favor infection. Spores are splashed from soil or foliage, so wet periods and overhead irrigation drive epidemics. **Treatment:** At first sign of spotting, apply broad-spectrum protectant fungicides preventatively. Chlorothalonil or mancozeb sprays every 7–10 days during wet weather will reduce spread. For organic control, use an OMRI-approved copper formulation. Remove affected leaves promptly and destroy them. If infection is severe, more aggressive measures (removing entire plants) may be needed. **Prevention:** Rotate tomatoes to non-host crops for at least 2 years to reduce soil inoculum. Completely remove all tomato debris from previous seasons (burn or deep bury it). Avoid planting tomatoes in the same spot year after year. Use mulches or plastic to prevent soil splashing on leaves, and stake/plumb plants to keep foliage off the ground. Ensure good spacing and ventilation. Control weeds (especially those in the nightshade family) around fields. Reduce leaf wetness: water at soil level in the morning so leaves dry quickly, and avoid working the crop when dew or rain is present. **Severity/Urgency:** Septoria leaf spot gradually reduces yields by defoliation. It rarely kills plants outright, but unmanaged spread can lead to significant leaf loss and sun-scald on fruit. It should be managed with preventative sprays; fungicides will not cure infected tissue. Timely intervention when spots first appear is needed to keep the disease from spreading through the crop.

## tomato___spider_mites

**Crop affected:** Tomato. **Symptoms:** Leaves show fine yellowish or white speckling (stippling) initially. Heavily infested leaves turn bronze or dusty-brown and may curl and dry up. Tiny fine webbing may appear on the undersides of leaves or between leaf joints under severe infestations. Infested plants become stunted and stressed, and their foliage can be wilted and dropping. Two-spotted spider mite (*Tetranychus urticae*) is most common, especially in hot, dry conditions. **Cause:** Spider mites are tiny arachnids (not insects) that feed on plant sap. They multiply explosively in hot, dry weather. Dusty or water-stressed plants favor mite outbreaks. Mites overwinter on plant debris or weeds and spread by wind or contact. **Treatment:** For small gardens, a strong jet of water can dislodge mites. Insecticidal soaps or neem oil (azadirachtin) sprays can control mites – be sure to cover leaf undersides and repeat every 7–10 days as needed. For severe infestations, use specific miticides (if available) such as abamectin at label rate (e.g. ~0.009–0.019 lb active ingredient per acre) or fenpyroximate. Always follow label directions regarding safety intervals. Applying horticultural or mineral oil (at 1–2% rate) can also smother eggs/mites, but avoid oil in very hot weather to prevent leaf burn. **Prevention:** Maintain crop health and humidity. Avoid dusty, excessively dry conditions and over-fertilizing with nitrogen (excess N encourages mite reproduction). Conserve natural predators: do not apply broad-spectrum insecticides (which kill predatory mites and insects). Use mulch and keep weeds down to reduce breeding sites. Scout regularly (look on leaf undersides) and act at the first sight of mites to prevent population boom. **Severity/Urgency:** Spider mites can build up quickly; large populations can defoliate tomato plants rapidly. It is important to intervene as soon as symptoms are seen. High urgency in hot, dry periods – if left unchecked, mites can seriously weaken or kill plants.

## tomato___target_spot

**Crop affected:** Tomato. **Symptoms:** On leaves and petioles, target spot lesions start as small brown flecks that expand into large (up to 1–2 cm) tan or gray circular spots with darker brown concentric rings (giving a “target” appearance). Surrounding tissue often becomes chlorotic (yellow). Leaves infected early can develop many lesions that coalesce, leading to heavy blighting and defoliation. Brown oval lesions can also form on stems. Fruit lesions begin as small brown sunken spots that enlarge and crack (often in a star or “X” pattern) during ripening. Severely spotted fruit often rot post-harvest. **Cause:** *Corynespora cassiicola* (syn. *Cercospora*) fungus causes target spot. It prefers warm (28–32°C) and very humid conditions; continuous leaf wetness (16–44 hr) is usually needed for infection. The pathogen produces spores throughout the season, spread by rain splash and wind. Dense canopies trap moisture and favor disease. **Treatment:** No curative action exists once spots develop. Preventive fungicide sprays are the key control. Use protectant fungicides like chlorothalonil or mancozeb early (apply before symptoms, then at 7–10 day intervals during humid weather). In trials, systemic fungicides (e.g. triazoles like difenoconazole, flutriafol; SDHIs like fluxapyroxad) and QoIs had variable effectiveness due to resistance. Rotate modes of action and include protectant chemistry. For organic management, copper hydroxide is an option (though control may be limited). Always cover the entire canopy (the fungus often hides inside). Remove and destroy infected debris at season’s end. **Prevention:** Rotate out of tomatoes and other Solanaceae (pepper, eggplant) for at least one season. Begin with healthy transplants (avoid fields or greenhouses with history of target spot). Weed control is critical – eliminate solanaceous weeds, volunteers, and plant residue that harbor the fungus. Provide good air circulation (prune and stake plants, don’t over-fertilize with nitrogen). Do not overhead irrigate; water at soil level and early in the day. **Severity/Urgency:** Target spot can cause rapid defoliation and fruit loss in hot, humid climates. In Florida it became “one of the most destructive” tomato diseases. In Sri Lanka’s tropical climate, it is a serious threat. Control is difficult once it takes hold, so management must be preventative. High urgency: treat with fungicides as soon as conditions are favorable or first symptoms appear. Without intervention, heavy losses of marketable fruit and severe defoliation will occur.

## tomato___yellow_leaf_curl_virus

**Crop affected:** Tomato. **Symptoms:** Infected plants are severely stunted and upright (‘bonsai’-like). New leaves are small, thickened and roll upward; leaves show pronounced crumpling and interveinal yellowing (chlorosis). The entire plant may appear bushy. Flowers often abort and fruit set fails. In heavy infections, yield can drop to near zero (100% loss is common when young plants are infected). These symptoms are very distinctive of Tomato yellow leaf curl virus (TYLCV). **Cause:** TYLCV is a DNA virus (genus *Begomovirus*) transmitted by the silverleaf/green whitefly (*Bemisia tabaci*). Whiteflies acquire the virus from infected plants (tomato or alternate hosts, including many weeds and some other crops) and then spread it as they feed. Warm tropical weather favors both virus spread and vector populations. **Treatment:** There is no cure for infected plants. Insecticide sprays (e.g. systemic imidacloprid or foliar pyrethroids/acephate) can reduce whitefly numbers but will not eliminate the virus if some whiteflies escape. Rogue infected plants immediately (especially in early outbreak) and burn them to reduce inoculum. Use reflective mulches or sticky traps to deter whiteflies. **Prevention:** Use TYLCV-resistant tomato varieties (with Ty-1, Ty-2 or Ty-3 resistance genes) where available. Always start with whitefly-free transplants. Cover young plants with insect-proof mesh (row covers) until flowering to keep out whiteflies. Time planting to follow any natural tomato-free period, reducing existing whitefly/virus loads. Control weeds (especially solanaceous weeds like nightshade and jimsonweed) near fields – these can harbor virus and whiteflies. Maintain strict sanitation: destroy all crop residue after harvest and remove volunteer tomatoes. If TYLCV is known in your area, practice an enforced regional host-free period (no tomatoes or other whitefly hosts) for several weeks to starve the virus and vector. Manage whiteflies actively: spray insecticides judiciously (rotate classes) and use oils or soaps for soft-bodied control, especially early in the crop cycle. **Severity/Urgency:** TYLCV is one of the most damaging tomato pathogens worldwide. Yield losses are often extreme (up to 100% loss) in fields with active infection. Even early symptoms warrant immediate action: remove infected plants and intensify vector control. Left unchecked, the virus can wipe out an entire crop. High urgency: protective measures (resistant varieties, vector management) are critical before and immediately after planting.
